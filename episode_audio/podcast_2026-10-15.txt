ALEX: Our third story. The Financial Stability Board — the FSB, which is the global watchdog that coordinates financial regulation across major economies — released final guidance yesterday on agentic AI in finance. And the headline inside the guidance is actually a deletion.
JAMIE: The deletion of "synthetic employees." Gone. Out of the document.
RUFUS: And I cannot tell you how pleased I am about that.
ALEX: Let's explain what we mean. Earlier draft versions of this guidance apparently treated AI agents — meaning AI systems that can plan and execute multi-step tasks with limited human oversight — as something like synthetic employees. The final version explicitly rejects that framing.
JAMIE: Because it's not just a bad metaphor. It's a dangerous one. If you call an AI an employee, you start thinking about it the way you think about employees — with expectations of judgment, of contextual understanding, of knowing when to stop.
RUFUS: And the FSB's point is that agents don't do that. They can interpret a goal in completely unexpected ways. The guidance explicitly states that AI agents do not reason like humans.
ALEX: So what does the final guidance actually require?
RUFUS: Risk-based triggers for human intervention. The idea is that financial firms need to define in advance — not in theory, but in their actual systems — the conditions under which a human has to approve what an AI agent is about to do. Particularly in high-risk automated workflows.
ALEX: Can you make that concrete? What does a risk-based trigger actually look like?
RUFUS: Think of it like a spending limit on a corporate card. Imagine — hypothetically — a bank's AI agent is instructed to rebalance a portfolio. You define in advance: any single transaction above a certain size, or any action in a particular asset class during a period of unusual volatility —
RUFUS: requires a human to sign off before execution. The agent doesn't decide whether the situation is unusual. The rule does.
JAMIE: That's actually a sensible way to think about it. The agent does the work up to the boundary, and then stops.
ALEX: And the FSB is saying banks need to build those boundaries into the code, not just the policy document.
RUFUS: That's the implication. Worth noting: the FSB is a global standard-setting body — this is a recommendation that shapes regulatory frameworks across major economies, not domestic law the moment it's published. The real test is whether it reaches the codebase or stays in the policy binder.
